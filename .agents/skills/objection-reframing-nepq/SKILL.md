# Objection Reframing (NEPQ-Style)

### When to use
When a prospect raises an objection, don't fight it or discount it. Use Jeremy Miner's NEPQ-style reframing to ask curious questions that let the prospect surface their own logic. Works in discovery, during demos, and in negotiation. Turns objections into discovery data.

### The framework

1. **Acknowledge without agreeing**: "That's a fair question / concern." Don't say "No, you're wrong" or "Actually, it's not that expensive."

2. **Ask a neutral question**: Redirect their objection back to them with genuine curiosity. Let them think through the issue.

3. **Listen to their reasoning**: Don't interrupt. They'll often talk themselves into the solution by explaining the objection aloud.

4. **Identify the real concern beneath the surface concern**: Price objections usually signal budget constraints, timing misalignment, or unclear ROI. "You're too expensive" often means "I don't see value yet." Push deeper.

5. **Reframe with their own words**: Use what they said to position next steps or alternatives. This feels collaborative, not sales-y.

### How to apply it

**Example 1: Price objection**

Prospect: "Your pricing is higher than [competitor name]."

Old (wrong) approach: "Actually, we're the same price if you factor in...": Defensive. Makes them explain why they're wrong.

NEPQ reframe:

**You:** "That makes sense. When you say higher, have you already decided what functionality you need, or are you still figuring out which features matter most to you?"

Prospect: "Well, we haven't fully mapped it out yet. We're mostly comparing on cost."

**You:** "Got it. If you did map out what you actually need, do you think the cost-per-feature picture would change?"

Prospect: "Probably. Once we know what we're really solving for, pricing might make more sense."

**You:** "Would it help to do that mapping before comparing pricing? That way you're not comparing apples and oranges."

Result: You've moved them from price-shopping to needs clarity. Objection dissolved, not fought.

---

**Example 2: Timeline objection**

Prospect: "We can't do this until next quarter. We're too busy right now."

Old approach: "I understand, but things won't get less busy. How about we start next month?": You're pushing. They're resisting harder.

NEPQ reframe:

**You:** "When you say you're too busy, is that a resource capacity issue, or is this project just not a priority compared to what's on your plate now?"

Prospect: "Both, honestly. We've got a system migration happening and it's eating up IT resources."

**You:** "How long is that migration?"

Prospect: "Six weeks, probably."

**You:** "So six weeks from now, would you have capacity to start a conversation about this, or is there something after the migration that would also keep you slammed?"

Prospect: "After the migration, things should calm down. Maybe early Q3."

**You:** "Got it. Let's aim for a quick 20-minute call the first week of Q3 to see if this still matters to you then. Fair?"

Result: You didn't pressure them. You mapped the real constraint (migration), identified the actual timeline window, and confirmed relevance at a future date. Objection answered without manipulation.

---

**Example 3: Internal buy-in objection**

Prospect: "I like this, but I need to get my finance team's sign-off, and they're going to have a lot of questions."

Old approach: "Sure, set up a call with finance. I'll handle their objections.": Risky. Finance often has unstated priorities or constraints.

NEPQ reframe:

**You:** "What do you think finance will want to understand first? Is it ROI, cost control, implementation time, or something else?"

Prospect: "Definitely ROI. And they'll want to know if there are any hidden costs."

**You:** "If you walked them through the ROI math using your specific use case, like how much time your team would save, do you think that changes their stance?"

Prospect: "Yeah, if I can show them we're saving 10 hours a week, they'll probably approve it."

**You:** "Perfect. Here's what I can send you: a one-pager that shows the time savings and the math on cost per hour saved. You can use that when you talk to finance. And if they have follow-up questions after that, you can loop me in. Does that work?"

Result: You've enlisted the prospect as your internal champion. You've given them a tool to sell internally. You haven't dismissed finance's concerns; you've acknowledged them and made it easy for the prospect to address them. Objection transformed into collaboration.

### Common traps

1. **Countering immediately**: You hear "We're happy with our current solution" and jump to "Well, our solution is different because..." You've started arguing, not asking. Prospect shuts down.

2. **Assuming the objection is the real issue**: "Too expensive" usually isn't really about price; it's about unclear value. "Not the right time" usually means priority, not calendar. If you address price without surfacing the real concern, you lose.

3. **Asking leading questions instead of neutral ones**: "Don't you think ROI is important?" is leading. They hear a close coming. Neutral version: "What would ROI look like for you?" lets them think.

4. **Not listening for silence**: You ask a question and they pause. You get uncomfortable and keep talking. NEPQ requires you to sit in the silence while they think. Silence = thinking = progress.

5. **Reframing with your words, not theirs**: You ask good questions but then answer for them. "So what you're really saying is..." no. Let them say what they mean.
